How it works: PCA learns what this product really looks like from the photos of all its listings (most sellers show the real thing), then rebuilds every photo from that look. A photo it cannot rebuild gets a low genuine-look %. Tested by slipping other products' photos in under this product's name: caught 98.4% of the time (95% CI 97.1–99.6%, 13 searches), while 7.8% of small shops' own listings were flagged (some of them are real copies). A copy made to look exactly like the original passes any photo check, so the radar adds the price: a less-known shop is in the risk zone when its photo does not look genuine or its price is more than 40% below the fair price.

Photo X-ray PCA on the product photos

Like "eigenfaces" for products. Every listing photo of this model is cut out of its background and shrunk to 48×48 pixels (6,912 numbers). PCA finds the average photo and the eigen-images: the directions in which these photos differ most. Each photo is then rebuilt from the main directions of the other photos; what cannot be rebuilt is shown as a heatmap, and the odd-one-out score (the same idea on DINOv2 features) flags photos unlike the rest: possibly another item, a used or fake product, or an accessory. Only less-known shops are warned about: an unusual photo at a big store is a photo choice, not a scam.

Tested on 48 real listings (results/json/photo_xray_test*.json): a photo of a different product slipped in was ranked the most unusual 95.8% of the time (chance 12.4%); a look-alike from the same search (for example the Pro Max among Pro listings) only 31.2% of the time. So: good at catching a wrong product, weak at telling close versions apart.

How it works

Machine learning decides which listings are the same product; price and seller are compared only afterwards.

1Understand the input

A name is searched as is. A store link gives the product name. A photo or screenshot goes to Google Lens, which names the product.

2Collect offers

Up to 40 offers from Indian online stores (Amazon, Flipkart, JioMart, Croma, brand stores and small shops) through Google Shopping, with title, photo, price and rating.

3Read photos and titles

Three neural networks turn each listing into numbers: CLIP (what the photo shows), DINOv2 (which exact object), MPNet (what the title means). PCA compresses them to 32 numbers each.

4Judge every pair

A logistic-regression model trained on 34,250 Shopee listings scores each pair: same item, same item in another colour/pack, or different. A model-word check stops "Pro" or "Gen 2" being mixed in.

5Sort the sellers

Among offers of the likely same item: the fair (median) price, the cheapest, and a sort order from match confidence, price fairness, rating, reviews and store reputation.

How accurate is it? Tested on 8 fresh products never used to build the rules (Prestige cooker, Philips trimmer, Mi power bank, Boroplus, Casio calculator, Sony headphones, Wipro bulb, Vaseline lotion): when DupeLens says two listings are the same item, it was the identical item in 23 of 40 pairs (57.5%; 95% CI 28.6–81.4%, resampling whole searches) and at least the same product line in 33 of 40 (82.5%; CI 63.9–94.3%); a random pair from the same search was the identical item only 3 of 30 times (10%). Without the hand-written rules the model alone was right about 26% of the time. Labelled blind by Claude, an AI assistant, not humans. Prices, ratings and links come live from Google Shopping (India) at search time. The matching model is DupeLens's own (trained on 34,250 Shopee listings, validation F1 0.864); the sort order is a fixed, transparent rule, not a trained model.
